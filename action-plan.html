Action Plan

The main paper lays out what each lever does and what it is worth. This page puts them in an order Congress and an administration could actually execute, and builds an illustrative year-by-year savings path from the figures already cited there.

1. Sequencing principles

The order below is set by execution feasibility, not urgency. Items that need no new legislation come first. Items that need one reconciliation-eligible revenue bill come next, since those need only a simple Senate majority. Annual appropriations items follow the normal budget calendar. The two hardest items, entitlement reform and immigration, come last, because both have historically required a standalone bipartisan deal rather than a routine vote. Financial repression sits outside the default sequence entirely, treated as a contingent fallback rather than a baseline step, given how disruptive it is and how much it depends on the Fed.

2. The roadmap

Phase 1 — Year 1

No new legislation required
Administrative / appropriations

Everything here can start immediately, because it is an executive, Treasury, or routine-appropriations action rather than a standalone bill.

  • IRS enforcement funding. An appropriations decision, not new authorizing legislation.
  • Gold reserve revaluation. A Treasury/Fed accounting action. One-time.
  • Liquidate seized bitcoin holdings. Requires formally reversing the 2025 Strategic Bitcoin Reserve executive order first. One-time.
  • Extend average debt maturity. A Treasury debt-management decision. This reduces refinancing risk rather than producing a scored dollar figure, so it does not appear in the table below, but it costs nothing to start.
  • Put tariff revenue on firm legal footing. Narrow legislation to replace the IEEPA authority the Supreme Court struck down, so revenue already being collected stops being clawed back in refunds.

Phase 2 — Years 2–3

One reconciliation bill
Budget reconciliation

These four are revenue measures that can be bundled into a single reconciliation bill, which needs only a simple Senate majority rather than 60 votes, the same vehicle used for major tax bills in 2017 and 2025.

  • Corporate rate 21% → 28%
  • Repeal stepped-up basis at death
  • Carbon tax
  • Raise the Social Security payroll tax cap

Phase 3 — Years 2–4

Normal appropriations cycle
Annual appropriations

Discretionary and defense reductions phase in gradually through the ordinary annual budget process rather than a single vote, realistically reaching full run-rate only after two or three budget cycles.

Phase 4 — Years 3–5

Standalone bipartisan deal
Commission-style negotiation

Social Security's benefit formula has been successfully reformed only once in the modern era, by the 1983 Greenspan Commission, which paired a Republican president and a Democratic House Speaker in a deal neither party could have passed alone. This paper assumes the same pattern: a negotiated package, the progressive price indexing formula from the main paper, Section 6.1, reached by Year 3 and effective for new beneficiaries from Year 4.

Its fiscal effect is deliberately left out of the year-by-year table below. Because only new beneficiaries are affected, savings ramp up over decades as the existing beneficiary population turns over, a path that requires SSA actuarial modeling this paper does not attempt to fabricate. See Section 6.1 of the main paper for the $25.1 trillion long-run shortfall and the reform's estimated 75-year effect.

Phase 5 — Years 4–6

Major standalone legislation
Immigration legislation

The formalized non-citizen worker visa program, main paper Section 9.2, is a large enough change that it needs its own bill and its own political coalition, independent of the fiscal package in Phases 1–3. Its growth effect is also left out of the table below: the $0.9 trillion figure in the main paper is the realized effect of the 2021–2024 immigration surge, not a forward score of this specific proposal, and reusing a historical result as a projection for a different policy would overstate what is actually known.

Contingent — Year 5 checkpoint onward

Only if the trajectory hasn't stabilized
Monetary policy, requires Fed cooperation

Financial repression, main paper Section 8.1, is held outside the default sequence. It requires sustained Federal Reserve cooperation in a way the other levers do not, transfers real cost onto savers and pension holders, and is measured in percentage points of debt-to-GDP rather than a dollar figure. That is also why it is left out of the table below, and out of the main paper's Section 11 total. Treat it as the option to revisit at a five-year checkpoint if Phases 1–4 have not stabilized the debt trajectory defined in Section 2 of the main paper.

Why this total ($6.3T) is larger than the main paper's Section 11 range ($3.6–5.6T): that range deliberately excluded the one-time gold and bitcoin items and the legally contested tariff revenue, to avoid overstating the structural, repeatable levers. This page's total adds those three back in, because an actual sequenced plan would use them, but they are a different kind of item. Two are one-time windfalls that cannot recur, and one, tariffs, depends on winning a legal fight the government has already partly lost once. Strip out gold ($740B), bitcoin ($29B), and tariffs (~$1.7T), and the remaining structural total over ten years is roughly $3.9 trillion, consistent with the main paper's range.

4. What this plan doesn't solve

Even at $6.3 trillion cumulative over ten years, this plan slows the deficit without reversing the underlying $40 trillion debt stock. It is a plan to stop the bleeding and start stabilizing the trajectory defined in Section 2 of the main paper, not a claim that the debt itself falls by this amount. Reversing the stock requires this plan to hold for multiple decades, the entitlement reform in Phase 4 to reach its full effect over a horizon much longer than the ten years modeled here, and sustained GDP growth that outpaces new borrowing.

The biggest risk to this sequence is political. Any one phase failing to pass does not just remove that phase's savings; it removes the precedent the next phase's negotiators are relying on. Phase 2's reconciliation bill is the most load-bearing step in the whole plan. It is the first real test of whether Congress will pass painful measures at all, and everything in Phases 3 through 5 assumes that test succeeds.
